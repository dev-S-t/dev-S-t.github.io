Line Design System — human-in-loop.dev

2026-09-28

Status

Draft 1. No site code changes until Sahil signs this doc off. Work lives on branch dev-s-t/design-system-line-based-74c703, cut from main at commit ab933df.

Already decided in Sahil's messages:

  • Lines are the whole concept. No single line model is locked in; every section uses lines differently.

Vision

The site is a field of dense black and white lines, and everything on it is made from those lines: their thickness, their spacing, or their absence.

  • The lines: concentric, parallel, uneven, randomly spaced, contour. They morph, gather around content, and part for it.

  • Non-uniform on purpose: density, weight and direction change across the page. Each section has its own line behaviour.

  • Made of lines: type, images, pointers, dividers and emblems are all drawn by lines, or by leaving lines out.

  • Alive, quietly: a slow rest motion, plus reactions to the pointer, to scrolling and to content moving.

  • Content wins: headings and components sit on calm ground, or look as if the lines formed them, like shapes seen in clouds.

  • Agent-first stays: the design adds visibility for search engines and LLMs and never removes any.

  • The bar: unique enough for design-award juries, with none of the AI-generated look.

The name already fits the concept. Contour lines are closed loops, and the domain is human-in-loop.dev. Reference image 1 shows a person standing inside concentric loops, so I suggest a human figure within the loops as the site's identity motif.

Controls every family exposes:

  • Spacing: 3 to 24 px, with random jitter and density maps.

  • Weight: 0.5 to 4 px, swelling and thinning along a line like an engraving.

  • Continuity: solid, cut at shapes, or fading out at the ends.

  • Waviness: from ruled straight to hand-drawn wobble.

  • Centre and direction: where rings start, which way flow runs.

  • Motion: drift speed, pointer response, scroll response.

Non-uniformity rules:

  1. Two neighbouring sections never share the same family with the same settings.

  2. Density varies inside every section: dense in empty areas, sparse around content.

  3. Every section uses at least two line weights.

  4. Tone comes only from lines. No grey fills, gradients or shadows.

  5. Every family also works as a still image, for fallbacks and reduced motion.

Theme and polarity

Light is the default theme: black lines on paper. Dark is light lines on ink. Any section can take the opposite polarity to whichever theme is active.

WhereLight theme (default)Dark theme
Normal sectionInk lines and text on paperPaper lines and text on ink
Inverted sectionPaper lines and text on inkInk lines and text on paper
Plate inside a sectionFilled with that section's groundFilled with that section's ground

Only two colours exist in the system; every grey you see is lines at some density. Proposed values, to confirm:

  • Ink: #0D0D0C, a near-black with a trace of warmth.

  • Paper: #F3F1EC, a warm off-white. Pure white under dense black hairlines glares and shimmers.

  • Contrast: ink on paper is about 17:1, far above the 4.5:1 WCAG AA minimum.

How it works in code:

  • Two theme tokens, --ink and --paper, swap when the theme changes.

  • Each section declares data-polarity="same" or "inverse". That sets its own --ground and --line, which its text, plates and line field read.

  • Selected text shows in the section's inverse colours, so even selection is ink and paper.

  • A theme switch in the header remembers the visitor's choice. Without JavaScript, the site shows the light theme.

A change of polarity between two sections is a design moment in itself: the cliff edge of reference 1, or ink flooding in along one contour line (see Sections, heights and seams).

Legibility toolkit

Every piece of content is made readable by one of six tools. Body text always gets a solid one: plate, clearing or glass.

ToolHow it worksUse it for
Sparse groundLines thin out and spread apart under large type; the type sits straight on themBig display headings
SilhouetteThe shape is solid ink or paper; lines stop a small gap before its edgeHero name, section numbers, figures
Plate (default)A rounded shape filled with the section's own ground; lines end at its edgeBody text, lists, FAQ, buttons
Liquid glassThe lines under the element bend at its edges and spread out in its middleFloating controls: nav, theme switch, resume download
ClearingLines part and press around content, with no hard edgePortrait, emblems, pull quotes
Line-formed typeLetters filled with hatching or contour lines, with a gap around themDisplay words only, as an accent

Plate details. The plate's colour follows its section's polarity, never the site theme. It has no border and no shadow. Its edge is drawn only by where the lines stop; lines may thicken slightly as they reach it.

Glass done properly. My first sketch only blurred the lines, which is why it looked wrong. Apple's Liquid Glass bends what is behind it. Here the page draws its own background, so the line shader can do the optics directly:

  • Bevel: lines kink sharply where they pass under the element's rounded edge.

  • Lens: in the middle the lines are magnified, so fewer and wider-spaced lines sit behind the text. That is what keeps glass readable here.

  • Rim light: a thin highlight along one edge, shifting slightly with the pointer.

  • Tint: a faint wash of the section's ground colour.

This works in every browser that runs the line field. The usual web approach, SVG filters inside backdrop-filter, only works in Chromium (kube.io write-up). Apple uses glass for the controls layer that floats above content, not for content itself (Liquid Glass); the site follows the same split.

Rules for all six tools:

  1. Text smaller than 24 px never sits on raw lines, and no line ever crosses a letter of body text.

  2. Contrast is checked against the worst case: the densest lines the text could overlap. Minimum 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text.

  3. Glass that cannot reach that contrast turns into a plate.

  4. Without JavaScript or WebGL, and when the visitor's system asks for reduced transparency, every glass element becomes a plate.

  5. Line-formed type is paint only. The real words stay in the HTML, selectable and indexable.

How fonts will load on the site:

  • Self-hosted WOFF2 files, trimmed to Latin characters, variable where the font allows.

  • font-display: swap with metric-matched fallbacks, so text shows at once and the layout does not jump.

  • Only the display font is preloaded. Target: under 150 KB of fonts per page.

Decision needed: one pairing, or a mix, after trying the lab.

Sections, heights and seams

On tablets and larger, every section is at least 88 percent of the screen height, and the hero is a full screen. A section with more content grows taller; nothing is ever clipped or put in an inner scroll box.

  • Heights use svh units, so mobile browser bars do not make sections jump.

  • Long sections, such as the nine projects, become chapters several screens tall, each with its own layout.

  • On phones, I recommend sections size to their content. A one-screen section holding four lines of text wastes space at that width.

  • No scroll-jacking and no forced snapping. The page scrolls normally, and seams react to scroll position.

The join between two sections is a seam. Each join picks one:

SeamKindWhat happensGood for
Straight cutHardLines stop dead on a clean horizontal edgeMoving between very different sections
Cliff edgeHardThe next ground rises along a rough contour line, as in reference 1Polarity flips
Rule bandHard3 to 7 parallel rules of different weights, like a banknote borderBefore the footer, around the resume
MorphFlowingOne line family turns into the next across the boundaryNeighbours with a related mood
FloodFlowingInk spreads along one contour line as you scroll, turning the section overEntering an inverted section
DiveFlowingConcentric rings pull you through a tunnel, as in reference 3Home into case studies
Pull-throughFlowingA few lines carry on and become the next section's structureParallel flow turning into timeline rails

Seams run on CSS scroll-driven animations where the browser supports them, with a small script elsewhere. Without either, each seam shows its finished still state.

Motion and interaction

The field is never still and never loud. It drifts slowly at rest, reacts locally to the pointer, and changes structure with scrolling and content.

TriggerWhat the lines doLimit
RestTerrain drifts. Rings breathe outward, flow lines slide, contours morph. A full visible change takes about a minuteSpeed is tied to time, never to frame rate
PointerRaises a small hill about 85 px across, and lines ring around it. Tilt fonts turn toward it; glass rim light shiftsThe normal cursor stays. No custom cursor dot or ring
ScrollTerrain moves slower than the content, for depth. Seams play by scroll positionContent is never held back; no scroll-jacking
ContentA block coming into view opens its clearing: lines part over about 400 ms. Hover and keyboard focus press lines in slightlyText is readable before any animation runs
TouchDragging leaves ripples; a tap sends out one ringNo tilt sensor, which iOS gates behind a permission prompt
Page changeCross-document view transitions carry the field from page to pageNeeds both pages to opt in; other browsers load normally
Reduced motionOne still frame. Seams show their end state; pointer effects and page transitions switch offFollows the visitor's system setting

Performance guard: if the frame rate stays under 45 fps for two seconds, the field renders at lower resolution, then freezes to a still frame. It pauses when the tab is hidden or the canvas is off-screen.

Browser support, as of September 2026:

  • Scroll-driven animations run in Chromium browsers and Safari 26; Firefox still has them off by default (MDN).

  • Cross-document view transitions run in Chromium browsers and Safari 18.2 and later; other browsers just navigate normally (CSS-Tricks).

Build approach

Plain HTML, CSS and one small script, with no framework, bundler or runtime dependencies. The Line Type Lab's shader is the starting point for the engine.

Files, all new, under the existing /assets folder:

  • assets/css/tokens.css: ink, paper, type scale, radii, @font-face.

  • assets/css/base.css: reset, typography, the site-wide components.

  • assets/css/sections/*.css: one file per section design. A page links only the section files it uses.

  • assets/js/lines.js: the line engine, loaded with defer.

  • assets/lines/*.svg: a still image of each section's field, used as the fallback.

  • assets/emblems/*.svg and assets/fonts/*.woff2.

Markup contract. A section declares its design in attributes, for example data-field="concentric", data-polarity="inverse" and data-seam="cliff". Elements that need calm ground carry data-legibility="plate", "glass", "clearing" or "sparse". Removing the script leaves valid, readable HTML.

Engine:

  • One fixed canvas and one WebGL2 context for the whole page. Browsers cap how many contexts a page may hold, so it is never one per section.

  • Each section is a scene: a line family plus its settings. At a seam, the shader blends the two neighbouring scenes.

  • The engine only measures plates and glass that are on screen, up to 16 per frame.

  • Pixel density is capped at 2 on desktop and 1.5 on phones. The engine pauses when the page is hidden.

Shared header and footer. The 23 designed pages (the 8 redirect pages stay untouched) repeat their head, header and footer, so any shared change goes to every file. I apply those edits with a one-off local script that is not committed, because everything in the repo is served publicly.
